# The simulator lab and core (theme/sim, Tools page)

Moved out of CLAUDE.md on 2026-10-05 so it only loads when someone works on this part. The rules in CLAUDE.md still apply.

## Working on the simulator without touching the site

`python3 lab.py` builds `lab.html` from `theme/widgets/qam-lab.html` (with the site's `.ground` glow layer under it, so glass reads as glass), serves it on 127.0.0.1:8823 and reloads the browser every time that widget, `theme/style.css` or `theme/app.js` is saved. The page carries an orange LAB bar saying how far the lab widget has drifted from the live one, and a button that opens phone and tablet frames of the same widget (loaded on demand, because each frame runs its own copy of the animation).

The live widget, `theme/widgets/qam.html`, is only written by `python3 lab.py --promote`, which copies the lab widget over it and rebuilds the site; until then the published simulator keeps serving whatever was last pushed. `--diff` shows what the lab has that the live one does not, `--reset` throws the lab copy away and starts again from live, `--build` renders `lab.html` once without serving. `lab.html` is generated and git-ignored; `qam-lab.html` is tracked so work in progress has a history and survives a `git clean`.

## The simulator core

`theme/sim/` is the second generation of the simulator, split so the physics can be tested without a browser. Add a model file to `SIM` in `lab.py` and to the list at the top of `simtest.js`. `core.js` is the namespace, a seeded RNG (mulberry32, so a screenshot and a test are reproducible) and `NFN.State`, which carries a tool's whole configuration in the URL hash as `#capacity/v1?bw=80&g=60,ax2,video;90,ac1,web`: a link is the argument. `rf.js` is propagation (free space, log distance, wall losses, noise floor, Fresnel, earth bulge, knife edge, antenna patterns from a half-power beamwidth, cell radius). `phy.js` computes rates from the constellation, coding rate and subcarrier count rather than looking them up, so matching the standard's table is a result: 2 streams of MCS 11 on 160 MHz comes out at 2402 Mb/s. `mac.js` is the tax: backoff, DIFS, SIFS, block ack, the cost of a retry, beacon overhead. `channels.js` counts the non-overlapping channels per band and width under US rules, DFS separated out because plenty of sites turn it off, and turns an AP count into a reuse factor. `capacity.js` turns a client mix into airtime and airtime into access points, and reports which of three ceilings binds: the airtime, the client count per radio, or the channels. The third one matters most. Splitting a load across more radios only helps while there are channels left to put them on, so the planner walks the whole range, finds where occupancy actually bottoms out, and says when a band cannot hold the load at all. That is why 400 clients on video will not fit in 2.4 GHz no matter how many radios are thrown at it, and why the same 300 clients need DFS switched on at 80 MHz in 5 GHz.

`node simsweep.js [count] [seed]` is the other half of the testing: hundreds of random sites across every profile, domain, band, width, kind, antenna, hill, tree line, crowd, hour, fade and measurement, each plan held to invariants that must be true whatever the numbers are (ceiling under uplink, demand and mesh; depths, parents, loops and hop ceilings; channels from the domain's list or none when it has none; link symmetry; more fade is less signal, taller masts never lose clearance, 20 MHz never has less SNR; EIRP inside the domain; no antenna beats its own gain; the picker never scores under all omnis; failing an AP never orphans it). It found one real defect on its first run, channel 0 handed out when a domain had no channel at that width, so run it after any change to `mesh.js`. 400 sites take about 40 seconds.

`aps.js` is the Aruba catalogue: per model the radios (streams, aggregate conducted power ceiling and per chain), the built in antenna kind (pole omni, ceiling down-tilt omni at 30 degrees, 90 by 90 directional, or connectors) and its peak gain per band, and a `verified` flag. Verified rows were read from HPE QuickSpecs HTML pages on 2026-09-12 (560, 570, 580, 670, 500, 510, 530, 550, 610, 630, 650, 730, 750; document ids in `src`); the Wi-Fi 5 rows and the 720 are PDF only and stand unverified with the figures as commonly published, flagged in the tool. `antennaFor()` registers a model's built in antenna in `mesh.ANTENNAS` as `m:<id>:<band>` with an elevation beamwidth derived from gain (101 over the directivity); `resolve()` gives a modelled AP its antennas, streams, power cap and standard, and a box without a radio on the backhaul band never links. A box with a 6 GHz radio or dual 5 GHz counts as having a radio to spare.

What a real Ekahau project taught the planner (read locally on 2026-09-12, never committed): antenna types carry two measured planes, 72 gains at 5 degree steps in absolute dBi with a `maxGain`; `registerPattern()` keeps them and `gainToward()` interpolates the planes and adds the two losses off the peaks (the textbook two-cut estimate; Ekahau's E plane reads 0 at the horizon, 90 up, 270 down). Wall types cost `attenuationFactor x thickness` dB per crossing per band (brick 33.33 x 0.3 is 10 dB, drywall 3, cinder block 5, concrete 12, a steel fire door 13, an elevator shaft 30) with a reflection coefficient and an 11 dB diffraction figure the planner does not yet use; `walls` are segments the links and the clients pay for (`wallsCrossed`). Requirements carry primary and secondary signal bars, SNR and rate; `coverage()` now reports primary and secondary (`coverage2`). The survey measurements themselves live in `.bin` files and are not read. `streamdeck/` holds a Stream Deck profile and icons for the keyboard editor.

`emit.js` turns a plan into the RF intent it implies (`intent()`: band, width, DFS, channel list, power window, mesh profile and ceiling, each AP's role, channel, power, antenna, compass aim and expected parent) and writes that intent for two controllers as bodies, not calls: `central()` gives ARM, a radio profile and per-AP `ap_settings` from the Configuration Swagger with the mesh cluster as CLI lines, `mist()` an RF template, the site, the mesh setting and each device as base or relay with a warning when the plan is deeper than Mist's one hop; `arubaChannel()` writes 149 at 80 MHz as `149E`. The "What to configure" fold shows `text()` and downloads either JSON. `central-pull.py` and `mist-pull.py` are the only things that talk to a controller, GET only, from the laptop, and write the same `site.json` (Central a measured path loss per pair, Mist an RRM RSSI); the planner's "Verify against Central or Mist" fold opens it.

`story.js` reads the `story` block `central-pull.py --story HOURS` writes (every client's trail, every radio's five minute noise and utilisation, reboots, AirMatch channel moves, the audit trail, client counts, bytes per AP) four ways and draws nothing: `bins`/`spikes`/`explain` turn roams into clients-that-moved per bin, find the spikes (three or more clients, two deviations over the busy mean) and pin each on what changed in the minutes before, saying whether it was the network (a reboot, a channel move re-associates everyone) or the people; `graph`/`whatIf`/`overlap` join radios by measured loss and mark the pairs that hear each other under 105 dB on overlapping channels (2.4 GHz within five, 5 and 6 GHz by the width's span), a what-if being a copy with one radio moved; `weather` is hour by day per radio with loud hours 6 dB over the median; `actual` turns bytes per sample into Mb/s; `clients` ranks who roams most and lands weakest. The "What happened" tool (`#story/v1`, fourth in the switcher) draws them: a timeline with event marks and a tap per bar, the graph per band with a move-this-radio what-if in the hash, a heat strip per radio, plan against actual with the planner's figures matched by AP name, a "Follow the cable" table that stops honestly at the AP's own port (the switch is New Central's on this tenant and Classic returns none), and the client list; the demo button opens `demo/mesh-story.json`. `transitions()` counts roams between named APs and the mesh planner draws them as arrows on the map (`rm` in its hash) once Verify has opened the same site.

`theme/widgets/waityourturn.html` is lesson 5's game, Wait Your Turn: EDCA contention played slot by slot. It is one self-contained file like the other games; the engine sits between `/* ENGINE START` and `ENGINE END */` with its timings and airtime copied from `mac.js` and `phy.js` (WMM AIFS and contention windows added, best effort waits 43 us where `mac.js` still uses DCF's 34). Levels play from curated seeds, and `node wyttest.js` (123 checks) proves the copy still matches `theme/sim`, that every level 2 seed is won by sixty human press timings, that no level 3 seed can be, and that level 4 has exactly one kind of answer. `root._wyt` is the test hook. Edit the widget in place and rerun the test.

`games.js` is the Academy's games with nothing drawn: `guess.round(seed, i)` (lesson 1's distances, then random, then a wall; truth is tx + gains - free space - wall) and `guess.score` (3 within a dB, 2 within 3, 1 within 6, streak up to x3); `fix.level(seed)` (a link failing at its rate), `fix.apply`, `fix.judge` (SNR by log distance n=3, walls, noise; retries grow as the margin shrinks; airtime by `mac.txopWithRetries`), `fix.best` (search over three moves) and `fix.score` (best over yours); `chan.level`, `chan.check`, `chan.fewest` (greedy colouring) and `chan.score`. Levels come from a mixed, warmed seed so small seeds spread. `theme/widgets/games-lab.html` (`lab.py games`) plays all three under the Academy theme with the game and seeds in the hash.

`kml.js` reads what Google Earth saves: a .kml or .kmz (the zip via `esx.js`), placemarks to masts (a height like "5 m" and the word "portal" in the name are honoured, so is a relative altitude), extruded polygons to buildings at their height, polygons or pins named "crowd 800" to crowds, the first path to the walk, and a `GroundOverlay` to the picture under the map at its own bounds (`img.box`). Projection is equirectangular about the centroid, north up. Google Earth's photographed buildings and terrain never export, and the tool says so. `demo/mesh-test.kml` is the fixture.

`theme/widgets/glass-lab.html` (`python3 lab.py glass`) is a prototype of a far heavier liquid glass than the site uses: refraction through an SVG turbulence field in `backdrop-filter: url()` (Chrome; Safari keeps the blur declared before it), a conic gradient rim, a chromatic fringe from two offset rings, a specular that follows the pointer, a light band that sweeps, motes drifting inside the panes, and meters that glow with motes of their own. It is a look to pick pieces from, not a replacement for `style.css`.

`esx.js` reads an Ekahau project: the .esx zip opened by hand (central directory, entries through `DecompressionStream("deflate-raw")`, no library), floor plans to fields by `metersPerUnit`, the surveyor's own APs to masts with height, direction (Ekahau's 0 is up the plan, so minus 90), tilt, power and channel width from the 5 GHz simulated radio, antennas matched to the planner's five by the words in their names, external antennas read as tri radio boxes, and the floor image to the underlay. `demo/mesh-test.esx` is a synthetic project the tests read. There is no export back to Ekahau: the .esx schema is theirs and a half-right file is worse than a CSV.

`node simtest.js` asserts all of it against figures that can be checked independently: free space at 1 m and 5.2 GHz is 46.76 dB, a 1500-byte packet at 6 Mb/s is 2096 us on air once the 54 bytes of 802.11 header, LLC/SNAP and CCMP are around it (2072 with the bare 34-byte textbook header), F1 at the middle of a 10 km link is 12.0 m, the earth bulge at the middle of 50 km is 36.8 m, and the PHY rates match the tables in 802.11n, ac, ax and be. Run it before promoting anything out of the lab. The counts in `channels.js` are US rules and are the first thing to change for another regulatory domain.

`venue.js` aims an antenna at a block of seats. A section is a trapezoid, which is how you count one standing in it: a front row, a back row and the rows between. From the mount height and the run back to the first row it gives every row a depression angle and a slant range, so the down-tilt, the beamwidth the section demands and the seats a given antenna actually lands on all fall out. `split()` answers the question that matters in a bowl, which is not whether one antenna fits but how to carve the section up: it tries block grids against the radio count capacity asked for and picks the one whose worst band needs the least antenna. The half power contour is the useful cell, not the last row that can hear anything, and the page says so.

`mesh.js` is the outdoor planner: APs on a flat field with masts, per-AP power, a portal or point role, a kind (`KINDS`: dual radio sharing the client radio for backhaul, tri radio with a dedicated backhaul, bridge unit serving no clients) and an antenna (`ANTENNAS`: omni, down-tilt omni, wide patch, narrow patch, highly directional, each with gain, both half power beamwidths, built-in tilt and front to back; `gainToward` is the cos^n fit in both planes). A directional antenna has an aim; an unset aim is resolved in two passes, the tree built once with the patch standing in as an omni to find the parent the geometry wants, then the patch turned toward that parent (portals toward their children) and the tree built again. Each end of a link contributes its gain toward the other, so a patch aimed wrong is a real loss. An antenna of `auto` (the default for new APs) is chosen by `suggest()`: coordinate descent from all omnis, trying every antenna on each undecided AP and keeping whatever makes `score()` best (megabits delivered, link headroom capped at twice the demand, 150 per unit of field coverage, 50 for the worst served AP, minus orphans, Fresnel trouble, single points of failure and a small tax on directional antennas), with a reason per AP against the omni it would otherwise wear. Pinned aims and hand-picked antennas are respected. The tool runs it on every change except mid-drag, where the last answer stands until the finger lifts. Coverage is the antenna's footprint at the target level (`contour`, `rssiAt` on a phone at chest height), not a circle. Obstacles are knife edges at their height, the ground itself as a knife edge when a mast is too short for the Fresnel zone (60% of F1 is the flag), a two ray ground reflection at rho 0.5 so mast height moves the fade, and the weaker transmitter setting a link's rate. `tree()` forms the way a mesh forms: every point attaches to the best node that already has a path, by the profile's metric, re-evaluating each round until the picture settles, with a sitting parent kept unless somebody is clearly better. `PROFILES` carry the metrics, shapes from the vendors' own documents (read 2026-09-11, cited in the file), numbers a sketch: Aruba `distributed-tree-rssi` (default) is a cumulative path cost, `linkCostRssi` doubling every 4 dB the link weakens plus a node cost per child the parent carries, so one marginal link costs more than two good hops but two near-equal hops do not beat one good direct link; Aruba `best-link-rssi` is literally the strongest link with a path; Cisco AWPP is adjusted ease, the least `ease` on the path divided by the hop count; Mist is single hop with a recommended four relays per base; 802.11s is the airtime metric summed along the path, the one that runs a whole row of APs straight back to the portal. Links under the profile's threshold are taken last. The backup parent is the best other candidate by the same metric; no backup is a single point of failure. The ground reflection is off by default for planning, because at these ranges the fade moves with a metre of mast and should be shown, not routed on. The ground is `ground()`, gaussian hills and hollows (`terrain`) under masts, links and obstacles; a tree line is porous (`foliage`, ITU-R P.833 through the canopy, or the knife edge over it, whichever is kinder); a `fade` margin comes off every budget, while `margin` only gates (a link counts once its SNR clears the lowest rate by that much, then runs at the rate `PHY.SNRMIN` gives its full SNR: a real point ran MCS 7 at SNR 25 to 30 on 2026-09-13 and the old margin-off-the-rate pick said 4); `nf` is the receiver noise figure (7 by default, and Verify replaces it with what the radios report over thermal, 3 on the home lab); `DOMAINS` cap EIRP by band so a bigger antenna means less power into it; each AP can pick its backhaul `band` and `bw` (different bands never link, the narrower end sets the width); a tri radio box carries `cant`/`caim`/`ctilt` for its clients beside `ant`/`aim`/`tilt` for the backhaul; a measured RSSI in `meas["i-j"]` replaces that link's model and teaches half the gap to each end's other links. `compass()` turns map azimuths into bearings given `north`, the bearing of the map's +x axis. `assignChannels()` gives each portal's tree one channel from `channels.list()` for the domain (a pinned `ch` on a portal wins) and `cochannel()` charges each link the busy share of the same-channel links whose ends it hears above the noise floor plus 6 dB, adjacent links excepted because the relay halving already pays for those. `assignClients()` spreads the loose clients evenly and puts each `crowd` (a headcount in a circle) on the loudest serving AP at twelve spots, counting the unserved; `CURVES` and `tod` scale the headcount by the hour. `plan()` shares every link among the APs behind it, halves the capacity at every relay on the path whose kind shares one radio between clients and backhaul (`relays` per AP), spreads the clients evenly over the reached APs, and then puts demand, what the mesh carries and the uplink on one scale so the ceiling is a picture. A measurement for a pair (`meas`) is an RSSI or a `{pl}` path loss and `measuredPrx()` turns either into received power, so what AirMatch reports can stand in for the model; `central-pull.py` is the only thing that talks to Central, from the laptop, GET only, and writes the `site.json` the planner's "Verify against Central" fold opens (on 10.8.1.0 the `show ap mesh link` RSSI column is a placeholder that reads 46 whatever the link; `show ap monitor ap-list` curr-rssi, curr-snr and pathloss are the honest reads, and Central's Tools > Commands runs them with no device login). `resilience()` fails each AP in turn and counts orphans, and with `pairs` every pair, worst first; `secondPortal()` tries every point as one more portal and judges it by the orphans across the whole sweep; `suggestPortal()` tries every mast as the only portal and as an extra one; `suggestHeights()` and `clearHeight()` say how much higher two masts must go to clear a link; `explain()` gives the budget for a link the tree did not choose and why; `narrate()` tells the whole tree as a story in the order a mesh forms (the profile's rule in one paragraph, the portals and whether they share a channel, each point by hop with every candidate it heard in the metric's own units, the parent it took, why the runner-up lost, its fallback or the lack of one, the relay halving behind it, and the ceiling), every figure read from the tree so it cannot drift from the map; the tool shows it in the "How this mesh formed" fold and retells it on every change; `gpsParse()` reads the text of `show ap gps ellipse` or `show ap gps summary` (latitude, longitude, altitude, the error ellipse's axes and angle, hop and distance for a fix inherited over a ranged neighbour, the chip state and constellations), `ftmParse()` reads `show ap range scanning-results` rows (peer BSSID, RTT in picoseconds, RSSI, standard deviation, valid RTTs) and `ftmMetres()` turns a round trip into a distance (6.67 ns is a metre), `geoPlace()` is the one equirectangular projection (about the centroid, north up, width and depth from their own spans) that the Verify fold, the kit and the KML reader all agree on; in the tool an AP's "Measured on site" fold takes the pasted GPS text, "Place masts from GPS" under "From the APs themselves" turns two or more fixes into positions and draws each fix's error ellipse on the map, and an FTM paste with the AP it came from becomes a measured distance against the planned one, peers matched to APs by radio MAC from a Verify import (a BSSID is the radio MAC with the low nibble counting VAPs). Fixes live on the AP object and in this browser only; the hash never carries a coordinate. All read from an AP-635 on 10.8.1.0, 2026-09-14; `walk()` reads a polyline as a client every few metres; `client()` associates a phone at chest height with the strongest AP and gives what it would get alone and with the crowd. `PROFILES` are behaviour sketches, not documentation: generic, Aruba style multi-hop portals and points, Mist style single hop base and relay. A point holds one parent at a time in all three, so two portals are failover and a split of the points, not a bonded link.

The tools live together in one lab page with a switcher. Each tool's State refuses the other's hash, so the address bar decides which one opens, and neither pushes its own hash on the first run: both boot before the switcher reads the hash, and an early push would stamp over the link somebody arrived with.

The tools built on it live in `theme/widgets/tools-lab.html`, worked on with `python3 lab.py tools`: the capacity planner, the seating block aiming tool, the mesh planner, the story tool, a sixth tab "Read it backwards" (`#readitbackwards`: a PHY rate in Mb/s in, every MCS row it could be out, same arithmetic as `phy.js`, added 2026-09-28 for Academy lesson 4), and a fifth tab, "On the air right now", which is the QAM simulator itself: the widget carries a `<!-- qam:here ... -->` slot and both `lab.py` (the lab copy, `qam-lab.html`) and `build-blog.py` (the live `qam` HTML with its data attributes) drop the simulator into it, so the Simulator pill could fold into Tools without the simulator leaving the tools page (`#air` opens that tab; the widget booted hidden sizes itself on the switcher's resize). The mesh map is SVG, redrawn whole on every change, with the drawing width following the box so labels are the same size on a phone as on a desk; a tool that boots hidden measures nothing, so the switcher fires a resize when it shows one. Pointer events do the dragging and the hash only updates when the finger lifts. The 3D toggle (`view=3d` in the hash, camera as `cam=yaw,pitch,zoom`, heights exaggerated by `zx`, layers as letters in `l3`) is a hand-rolled perspective camera on an orbit around the field centre, drawing the same plan in painter's order: ground grid and footprints first, then obstacles as lit twelve sided prisms, links between mast tops with the 60% Fresnel zone as rings that turn orange where they clip the ground or an obstacle, the two ray bounce point with its gain, masts with the antenna pattern as a wire surface (30 dB of range, lobe metres divided by the exaggeration so the shape stays a shape), and the client. No library: fifty shapes do not need WebGL, and the site has never loaded one. Drag the ground to orbit, wheel or pinch to zoom, tap a mast top to select, drag a mast top to move it (`unproject3` puts the pointer on the ground plane under the mast). The tool also holds: hills, crowds and a walk as map objects with their own modes; a clock (curve and hour, with play); a site image under the field kept in localStorage with a two-tap scale; GPS placement (the first fix is the middle of the field, kept in localStorage) and a compass aim; per-AP measured RSSI inputs; a "link from here" explainer; JSON and CSV export and import; A/B scenarios in localStorage with a compare table; an install sheet with compass bearings. The expensive advisors run only when the picture is still (`adviceKey`). The look is the liquid glass from `glass-lab.html`, now on the tool itself: every `.t-panel` and the `.m-mapwrap` around the map refract the page through `#tg-refract` (Chrome; Safari keeps the blur), wear the conic rim and the chromatic fringe, and carry a `.lg-spec` with a pointer-following specular, a sweeping band and motes; pills, chips, selects and the details folds are thin panes; sliders sit in sunk glass tracks with glossy knobs; three `.lg-blob` lights drift in the `.ground` layer (the tool creates the layer if the page lacks it). Panels rise in with the site's stagger, nodes are lit orbs with a soft halo, links carry a bloom under the line and a pulse riding from the portal at a speed set by the rate, portals breathe. The 3D view draws the field as lit cells that follow the hills with depth fog toward the far edge, obstacles as shaded prisms with soft shadows, masts with shadows and orb heads, antenna lobes as lit translucent surfaces, Fresnel tubes with a ribbon body, and crowds as standing figures. Every animation is SMIL on opacity, radius or motion path, and all of it stops under reduced motion (`MOTION`). The layout is progressive: what a tap does is one select at the top of the map; the AP panel shows role, antenna, mast and fail, with aim, tilt, radio, the link explainer and measurements behind `<details class="m-more">`; the site panel shows size, uplink, clients, application and ground, with radios, rules, the image, Ekahau and the phone behind details; on the answer side only the big number, stats, ceiling bar, flags and advice are open by default. It is on the site as `tools.html` (nav pill "Tools") since 2026-09-14: `build-blog.py` concatenates the `theme/sim/*.js` files in `lab.py`'s `SIM` order into one `<script>` and inlines the promoted `theme/widgets/tools.html` under a `.sim-intro` head, with an OG card and a sitemap entry; `python3 lab.py tools --promote` is the only thing that writes that widget, so the lab keeps moving while the site serves the last promote. In the lab the model files load as separate scripts for a fast reload. Six nav pills fit a 360 px phone only because the header gives up its gutters and the pills their padding under 700 px, and the nav scrolls sideways rather than clipping if a seventh ever arrives.

`python3 capture.py` regenerates the figures the posts take from the simulator, so a tool change is a re-run rather than a fresh round of screenshotting. Each shot drives `simulator.html` in a headless browser, entirely by script rather than by clicking, because a click scrolls the control into view and a recording then starts somewhere down the page. `academy-01` writes `media/academy-01-six-db.png` (four link budget strips at the lesson's own lab distances, 2, 4, 8 and 16 m, free space, omni, captured at devicePixelRatio 2 and shown at half size) and `media/academy-01-six-db.mp4` plus its poster. The step between the strips is read back from `root._dbg().lb`, which reports the widget's own distance, received power, SNR, gain toward the client and noise floor, and the crop comes from `root._dbg().strip`, the panel rectangle in canvas units: neither number is worked out a second time in the capture script, so the figure cannot drift from the tool. Add a shot by adding a function to `SHOTS`.

`PYTHONPATH=.qa/pylib python3 regress.py` is the visual regression harness (it serves the repo itself on a free port and finds the same Chromium the QA bot uses; exit 1 on a section A failure): every page including `tools.html` and `kit.html` at 360, 768 and 1280, asserting no horizontal scroll, no page errors, no broken images, the mascot fixed at z-index 60, at least 140 px of clearance at the end of the content (page padding plus main padding, since the padding moved to `main` when the footer was pinned to the bottom), at most one green call to action per view, no contact copy outside the socials page, and no tap target under 38 px.
